\section{The Three Protocols}
\label{sec:three-protocols}

\subsection{OpenBCI Cyton}

A wireless EEG streaming protocol using RFDuino radio modules. The Cyton board transmits to a USB dongle over 2.4\,GHz radio, which presents as a virtual serial port.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{8 EEG channels} at 24-bit resolution (ADS1299)
    \item \textbf{3-axis accelerometer} (LIS3DH)
    \item \textbf{250\,Hz} sample rate (limited by radio bandwidth)
    \item \textbf{Wireless} link (2.4\,GHz Nordic Gazelle protocol)
    \item \textbf{No checksum} --- relies on USB's built-in error detection
    \item Max radio payload is 31 bytes; dongle adds 2 framing bytes $\rightarrow$ 33 bytes
\end{itemize}

\subsection{InEar Teensy Original}

A wired USB protocol for a Teensy~4.1 microcontroller with ADS1299 EEG front-end plus multiple biometric sensors. Designed for in-ear EEG devices.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{5 EEG channels} at 24-bit resolution
    \item \textbf{9 auxiliary channels}: accelerometer (3), PPG heart sensor (3), temperature, battery, sync
    \item \textbf{1000\,Hz} sample rate
    \item \textbf{Fixed 56-byte packets} --- every packet carries ALL sensor data
    \item \textbf{13 bytes of padding} reserved for future use (wasted bandwidth)
    \item XOR checksum for error detection
\end{itemize}

\subsection{InEar Teensy Optimized}

Same hardware as the original, but with a redesigned protocol that sends sensor data only at each sensor's native rate:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Variable-length packets} (26--55 bytes) based on content
    \item \textbf{Multi-rate sampling}: EEG at 1000\,Hz, accelerometer at 250\,Hz, PPG at 100\,Hz, temperature/battery at 10\,Hz
    \item \textbf{Enumerated packet types} tell the receiver exactly what data is included
    \item \textbf{Sample-and-hold} on the receiver: when slow sensors aren't sent, the receiver uses the last known value
    \item Same error detection (XOR checksum)
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The PPG Problem}

PPG (photoplethysmography / heart pulse) data is the single biggest auxiliary payload:

\[
\text{PPG} = 3 \text{ channels} \times 6 \text{ bytes} = 18 \text{ bytes per sample}
\]

That is \textbf{more than all 5 EEG channels combined} (15 bytes)!

In the original protocol, 18 bytes sent $1000\times$/sec = \textbf{18,000\,B/s just for PPG}.

The heart pulse signal has meaningful content up to ${\sim}$25\,Hz. Nyquist requires $\geq$50\,Hz sampling. The optimized protocol sends PPG at 100\,Hz ($2\times$ Nyquist margin):

\begin{align}
\text{Original PPG:}\quad & 18 \text{\,B} \times 1000 \text{\,Hz} = 18{,}000 \text{\,B/s} \nonumber\\
\text{Optimized PPG:}\quad & 18 \text{\,B} \times 100 \text{\,Hz} = 1{,}800 \text{\,B/s} \nonumber\\
\text{Savings:}\quad & 16{,}200 \text{\,B/s}\;(90\% \text{ reduction for PPG alone!}) \nonumber
\end{align}

\subsection{If OpenBCI Had To Carry The Same Data}

If OpenBCI Cyton needed to send the same sensor suite as InEar at 1000\,Hz:

\begin{align}
\text{Needed per sample:}\quad & 15 + 6 + 18 + 2 + 2 + 5 = 48 \text{\,bytes} \nonumber\\
\text{Needed per second:}\quad & 48 \times 1000 = 48{,}000 \text{\,B/s} \nonumber\\
\text{Available (8-N-1):}\quad & 115{,}200 \div 10 = 11{,}520 \text{\,B/s max} \nonumber
\end{align}

\textbf{Note to self:} OpenBCI would need $4.2\times$ more bandwidth than available. This is why direct OpenBCI-vs-InEar bandwidth comparisons are misleading --- they solve fundamentally different problems with fundamentally different hardware.

\section{Bandwidth Analysis}
\label{sec:bandwidth}

\subsection{Serial 8-N-1 Basics}

All three protocols use standard serial (UART) with 8-N-1 encoding. Each byte requires 10 bits on the wire:

\[
\underbrace{1}_{\text{start}} + \underbrace{8}_{\text{data}} + \underbrace{0}_{\text{parity}} + \underbrace{1}_{\text{stop}} = 10 \text{ bits per byte}
\]

Therefore:
\[
\text{Max bytes/sec} = \frac{\text{Baud rate}}{10} \quad(\text{NOT } \div 8\text{!})
\]

\section{Optimization Tactic Details (All 28 Tactics)}
\label{sec:tactic-details}

\subsection{Category 1 --- Bandwidth Optimization}

% --- 1A ---
\subsubsection{1A --- Multi-Rate Auxiliary Sampling \implemented}

Instead of sending every sensor at 1000\,Hz, send each at a rate matching its actual information content (Nyquist rate).

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{|l|r|r|r|r|}
\hline
\textbf{Sensor} & \textbf{BW of Interest} & \textbf{Nyquist Min} & \textbf{Send Rate} & \textbf{Oversampling} \\\hline
\hline
EEG & 0.1--300\,Hz & 600\,Hz & 1000\,Hz & $1.67\times$ \\
Accelerometer & 0--100\,Hz & 200\,Hz & 250\,Hz & $1.25\times$ \\
PPG & 0.5--25\,Hz & 50\,Hz & 100\,Hz & $2\times$ \\
Temperature & ${\sim}$0\,Hz (DC) & ${\sim}$1\,Hz & 10\,Hz & $10\times$ \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\caption{Nyquist justification for multi-rate scheduling}
\end{table}

\textbf{Physics justification}: Temperature changes over seconds, not milliseconds. Sending temperature $1000\times$/sec is like checking the weather every 3.6 seconds --- you'll get the same reading.

\textbf{Trade-off}: Parser complexity rises from 1 packet type to 14 (7 base $\times$ 2 with marker flag). Receiver must implement sample-and-hold. If a PPG packet is lost, stale for 10\,ms instead of 1\,ms.

\textbf{Impact}: Saves ${\sim}$49\% bandwidth ($56{,}000 \to {\sim}28{,}500$\,B/s).

% --- 1B ---
\subsubsection{1B --- Delta Encoding for EEG}

Send the first sample as an absolute 24-bit value, then send only the \textit{difference} between consecutive samples using fewer bits. EEG at 1\,kHz changes very little sample-to-sample; a typical delta fits in 8--12 bits vs.\ 24 bits.

\begin{lstlisting}[style=diagram, caption={Delta encoding example}]
Absolute:  sample1 = 123456    -> 3 bytes (24-bit)
           sample2 = 123489    -> 3 bytes (24-bit)
           sample3 = 123501    -> 3 bytes (24-bit)

Delta:     sample1 = 123456    -> 3 bytes (24-bit, keyframe)
           d2 = +33            -> 1 byte  (8-bit delta)
           d3 = +12            -> 1 byte  (8-bit delta)

Savings: 9 bytes -> 5 bytes for 3 samples (44%)
\end{lstlisting}

\textbf{Note to self:} If you lose ONE packet, every subsequent sample is corrupted until the next keyframe. Requires periodic absolute keyframes (e.g., every 100th sample) and loss detection via sequence number gaps. Potential savings: 40--60\% for EEG data specifically.

% --- 1C ---
\subsubsection{1C --- Variable-Width Integers (VarInt)}

Encode values using a variable number of bytes --- small values use fewer bytes (like Protocol Buffers).

\textbf{Trade-off}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item[\ding{55}] Packets have \textbf{unpredictable size} --- parser can't jump to fixed offsets
    \item[\ding{55}] Bit-level parsing is expensive
    \item[\ding{55}] Cannot pre-validate packet size
    \item[\ding{51}] Average savings of ${\sim}$30\% combined with delta encoding
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Verdict}: High complexity for moderate savings. Better suited for file formats than real-time streams.

% --- 1D ---
\subsubsection{1D --- Eliminate Reserved/Padding Bytes \implemented}

The original protocol has 13 reserved bytes in every packet, filled with zeros. The optimized protocol removes them entirely.

\[
13 \text{\,bytes} \times 1000 \text{\,packets/sec} = 13{,}000 \text{\,B/s} = 130{,}000 \text{\,bits/sec} \;\textbf{WASTED}
\]

\textbf{Trade-off}: Removing reserved bytes means future sensors require defining a new packet type (which the optimized protocol already supports).

\textbf{Impact}: Saves 13,000\,B/s --- more than the entire accelerometer data stream.

% --- 1E ---
\subsubsection{1E --- PPG to 24-bit (from 48-bit) \freewin}

PPG is currently encoded as 48-bit (6 bytes/channel). Most PPG ADCs are 18--20\,bit. Using 24-bit (3 bytes) is more than enough.

\begin{align}
\text{Current:} \quad & 3 \text{ channels} \times 6 \text{\,B} = 18 \text{\,B/sample} \nonumber\\
\text{Proposed:} \quad & 3 \text{ channels} \times 3 \text{\,B} = 9 \text{\,B/sample} \nonumber\\
\text{Savings:} \quad & 9 \text{\,B per PPG packet} \nonumber
\end{align}

The MAX30102 PPG sensor has an 18-bit ADC. The firmware packs this into 48 bits using \texttt{packInt48BE()}, leaving the top 3 bytes always zero. 24-bit gives 16.7 million levels of precision for a signal with ${\sim}$18 bits of useful information.

\textbf{Trade-off}: Essentially none. This is a \textbf{free win}.

% --- 1F ---
\subsubsection{1F --- Smaller Header/Footer}

Reduce the header and/or footer byte count. Current: 2-byte header (\hexval{A5}\,\hexval{5A}) + 2-byte footer (\hexval{C0}\,\hexval{C0}) = 4\,bytes/packet.

\textbf{False sync math}: With a 2-byte header, false sync probability is $1/65{,}536$. With 1-byte: $1/256$ --- $256\times$ more likely.

\textbf{Verdict}: Marginal savings (1--3\,B/pkt) with meaningful reliability impact. Not recommended.

% --- 1G ---
\subsubsection{1G --- Multi-Sample Batching}

Pack $N$ samples into a single packet, amortizing header/footer/checksum overhead.

\begin{align}
\text{Current (1/pkt):} \quad & 3 \times 26 = 78 \text{\,B for 3 samples, 33\,B overhead (42\%)} \nonumber\\
\text{Batched (3/pkt):} \quad & 8 + 3 \times 15 + 3 = 56 \text{\,B for 3 samples, 11\,B overhead (20\%)} \nonumber
\end{align}

\textbf{Trade-off}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item[\ding{55}] Latency increases by $N\times$ (at 1\,kHz with $N=4$: 4\,ms instead of 1\,ms)
    \item[\ding{55}] Losing one packet loses $N$ samples
    \item[\ding{51}] At $N=4$, overhead drops from 42\% to 17\%
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Verdict}: Poor fit for real-time neurofeedback; good for high-latency-tolerant recording.

% --- 2C ---
\subsubsection{2C --- USB Flush Optimization}

Use \texttt{Serial.send\_now()} to force immediate USB packet transmission instead of waiting for the USB stack's default ${\sim}$1\,ms buffering interval.

\begin{lstlisting}[style=cpp, caption={USB flush optimization}]
Serial.write(packet, len);
Serial.send_now();  // Force immediate USB transfer
// USB packet sent on next USB frame (~125 us for USB 2.0 HS)
\end{lstlisting}

\textbf{Verdict}: Easy win --- single line of code, removes up to 1\,ms of non-deterministic latency. Currently \textbf{not implemented} in the firmware.

% --- 2D ---
\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{2D --- DMA SPI\textrightarrow UART Pipeline}{2D --- DMA SPI to UART Pipeline}}

Use Direct Memory Access hardware to transfer data from ADS1299 SPI bus directly to UART without CPU involvement.

\begin{lstlisting}[style=diagram, caption={CPU-mediated vs.\ DMA data flow}]
Current:  ADS1299 --SPI--> [CPU reads] --> [CPU builds] --> [CPU writes UART]
DMA:      ADS1299 --SPI--> [DMA->RAM]  --> [CPU builds] --> [DMA->UART]
                            ^ HW only ^                      ^ HW only ^
\end{lstlisting}

\textbf{Verdict}: High-effort, high-reward. Best suited if Teensy needs additional processing alongside streaming.

\subsection{Category 3 --- Integrity Optimization}

% --- 3A ---
\subsubsection{3A --- CRC-8 Instead of XOR \freewin}

Same 1-byte cost, dramatically better error detection.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{|l|c|c|}
\hline
\textbf{Error Type} & \textbf{XOR} & \textbf{CRC-8} \\\hline
\hline
Single-bit errors & 100\% & 100\% \\
2-bit errors & 100\% & 100\% \\
Odd \# of bit errors & 100\% & 100\% \\
Even \# of bit errors & \textbf{0\%!!} & 99.6\% \\
Burst errors ($\leq$8 bit) & ${\sim}$50\% & 100\% \\
Random errors & ${\sim}$50\% & 99.6\% \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\caption{Error detection: XOR vs.\ CRC-8}
\end{table}

CRC-8 requires a 256-byte lookup table and one table lookup per byte. At 56,000 lookups/sec --- negligible on a 600\,MHz Teensy or any modern PC.

% --- 3B ---
\subsubsection{3B --- Forward Error Correction (FEC)}

Add redundancy bytes for error correction without retransmission (Hamming, Reed-Solomon).

\textbf{Verdict}: Overkill for wired USB. USB~2.0 has hardware CRC16 on every packet. Only relevant for Bluetooth/WiFi migration.

% --- 3C ---
\subsubsection{3C --- Retransmission (ARQ)}

Receiver sends NACK on bad packets; firmware re-sends.

\textbf{Trade-off}: Adds 2--3\,ms latency per retransmit, requires bidirectional protocol and TX ring buffer on Teensy. On USB with near-zero loss rate: unnecessary.

% --- 3D ---
\subsubsection{3D --- Longer Sync Pattern}

Use 3--4 byte sync instead of current 2-byte \hexval{A5}\,\hexval{5A}.

\begin{align}
\text{2-byte:} \quad & P(\text{false sync}) = 1/2^{16} = 1/65{,}536 \nonumber\\
\text{3-byte:} \quad & P(\text{false sync}) = 1/2^{24} = 1/16{,}777{,}216 \nonumber\\
\text{4-byte:} \quad & P(\text{false sync}) = 1/2^{32} \approx 1/4.3 \times 10^9 \nonumber
\end{align}

\textbf{Verdict}: Moderate value. Current 2-byte + checksum verification is already quite robust.

% --- 3E ---
\subsubsection{3E --- Explicit Length Field}

Add a 1-byte length field so the parser knows the exact packet size before reading.

\textbf{Trade-off}: +1\,byte/packet (1,000\,B/s) for forward-compatibility --- host can skip unknown packet types gracefully without a lookup table.

\textbf{Verdict}: Excellent trade-off. Almost always worth it in evolving protocols.

\subsection{Category 4 --- Parser/CPU Optimization (Host Side)}

% --- 4A ---
\subsubsection{4A --- Ring Buffer Instead of \texttt{std::deque} \freewin}

The current parser uses \texttt{std::deque<uint8\_t>}. Each \texttt{push\_back()}/\texttt{pop\_front()} may allocate heap memory. At 28,500\,bytes/sec $\rightarrow$ up to 28,500 allocations/sec with poor cache locality.

A fixed 16\,KB circular buffer eliminates all allocations during operation:

\begin{lstlisting}[style=cpp, caption={Ring buffer implementation sketch}]
class RingBuffer {
    uint8_t buffer[16384];  // 16 KB, power of 2
    size_t head = 0, tail = 0;
public:
    void push(const uint8_t* data, size_t len) {
        for (size_t i = 0; i < len; i++)
            buffer[(tail++) & 0x3FFF] = data[i];
    }
    uint8_t operator[](size_t i) const {
        return buffer[(head + i) & 0x3FFF];
    }
    void consume(size_t n) { head += n; }
    size_t available() const { return tail - head; }
};
\end{lstlisting}

Zero allocation hot path, better cache performance, same semantics.

% --- 4B ---
\subsubsection{4B --- Zero-Copy Parsing}

Parse values directly from the ring buffer instead of copying into a temporary struct first.

\textbf{Trade-off}: Must handle ring buffer wrap-around mid-packet. Saves one memcpy per packet (56\,B $\times$ 1000/sec = 56\,KB/s of copying eliminated). Most impactful combined with 4A.

% --- 4C ---
\subsubsection{4C --- SIMD Byte Conversion}

Convert 24-bit big-endian $\to$ 32-bit signed integers using SSE/AVX (x86) or NEON (ARM) in parallel.

\textbf{Verdict}: Micro-optimization. Only 5 channels --- sequential code takes nanoseconds. Not worth the platform-specific complexity.

% --- 4D ---
\subsubsection{4D --- Remove \texttt{std::chrono} from Hot Path \freewin}

The parser calls \texttt{std::chrono::steady\_clock::now()} on every \texttt{updateBuffer()} for bandwidth monitoring. On Windows this involves a kernel syscall.

\textbf{Fix}: Replace with a simple packet counter check (\texttt{if (count \% 5000 == 0)}).

\textbf{Effort}: ${\sim}$10 minutes. Replace 3--4 lines. \textbf{Easiest free win} in the entire taxonomy.

% --- 4E ---
\subsubsection{4E --- Batch \texttt{addToBuffer()} Calls}

Currently 1 call per packet = 1000 lock cycles/sec. Batching $N$ samples reduces to $1000/N$ lock cycles.

\textbf{Trade-off}: Adds up to $N$\,ms of batching latency. Good for throughput, bad for real-time latency.

\subsection{Category 5 --- Firmware CPU Optimization (Teensy Side)}

% --- 5A ---
\subsubsection{5A --- Precomputed Packet Templates}

Pre-fill static fields (header, footer, type) at startup into 7 template buffers. In ISR: \texttt{memcpy} template, overwrite only dynamic fields.

\textbf{Trade-off}: Uses $7 \times 55 = 385$\,bytes of RAM. Teensy~4.1 has 1\,MB. Negligible.

% --- 5B ---
\subsubsection{5B --- Packet Type Lookup Table \freewin}

Replace cascading modulo operations with a precomputed 1000-entry array.

\begin{lstlisting}[style=cpp, caption={Packet type lookup table}]
// Current: 5 modulo operations + 5 branches
uint8_t getPacketType(uint32_t n) {
    if (n % 1000 == 0) return 0x06;
    if (n % 100 == 0)  return 0x04;
    if (n % 20 == 0)   return 0x03;
    if (n % 10 == 0)   return 0x02;
    if (n % 4 == 0)    return 0x01;
    return 0x00;
}

// Proposed: 1 modulo + 1 array read
static uint8_t typeTable[1000];  // precomputed at startup
uint8_t type = typeTable[sampleNum % 1000];
\end{lstlisting}

Uses 1000\,bytes of RAM. Teensy has 1\,MB. \textbf{Free win}.

% --- 5C ---
\subsubsection{5C --- ISR-Based Packet Assembly}

Build packets inside the IntervalTimer ISR for deterministic timing.

\textbf{Current status}: The existing firmware already does this (builds and sends in ISR callback). \textbf{Already implemented}.

\subsection{Category 6 --- Robustness Optimization}

% --- 6A ---
\subsubsection{6A --- Heartbeat / Watchdog}

Firmware sends a periodic ``I'm alive'' packet at ${\sim}$1\,Hz. If host doesn't receive it for $N$ seconds $\rightarrow$ connection lost.

\begin{lstlisting}[style=diagram, caption={Heartbeat disconnect detection}]
Normal:   Host <--- [Data] [Data] [HB] [Data] --- Teensy

Failure:  Host <--- [Data] [Data]             --- (cable yanked!)
          ...3 seconds, no heartbeat...
          HOST: CONNECTION LOST -> show error, clean up
\end{lstlisting}

\textbf{Verdict}: Excellent addition. ${\sim}$10\,B/s overhead for fast disconnect detection.

% --- 6B ---
\subsubsection{6B --- Connection Handshake}

Proper startup: \texttt{HELLO} $\to$ Device Info $\to$ \texttt{START} $\to$ streaming $\to$ \texttt{STOP} $\to$ \texttt{OK}.

\textbf{Benefits}: Auto-detect firmware version/channel count/sample rate. Prevents protocol mismatch. Clean startup and shutdown.

\textbf{Trade-off}: Adds ${\sim}$100\,ms to connection startup.

\subsubsection{7A --- Hardware Clock Synchronization}

Synchronize Teensy's crystal with host PC clock. Current drift: ${\sim}$50\,ppm $\rightarrow$ 180\,ms after 1 hour.

\textbf{Method}: Periodic NTP-style time exchange ($T_1, T_2, T_3, T_4$):
\[
\text{Offset} = \frac{(T_2 - T_1) + (T_3 - T_4)}{2}, \qquad
\text{RTT} = (T_4 - T_1) - (T_3 - T_2)
\]

After correction: timestamps accurate to ${\sim}100\,\mu$s. Critical for multi-device synchronization.

\textbf{Note to self:} OpenBCI's 71.6\% utilization is not ``worse'' --- it's operating near the physical limit of its wireless radio. It can't go faster without changing hardware. The InEar protocols have abundant headroom because USB is orders of magnitude faster than a 2.4\,GHz radio link.

\textbf{If OpenBCI had to carry InEar's data}, it would need $>4\times$ its available bandwidth --- physically impossible without hardware changes.

\section{Appendix: Serial 8-N-1 Bandwidth Math}
\label{sec:appendix-serial-math}

{\sloppy A common mistake in serial bandwidth calculations is using
\texttt{baud\_rate~/~8} instead of \texttt{baud\_rate~/~10}.\par}

\subsection{What ``8-N-1'' Means}

\begin{align}
8 &= 8 \text{ data bits per character} \nonumber\\
\text{N} &= \text{No parity bit} \nonumber\\
1 &= 1 \text{ stop bit} \nonumber\\[1em]
\text{Total:} \quad & \underbrace{1}_{\text{start}} + \underbrace{8}_{\text{data}} + \underbrace{0}_{\text{parity}} + \underbrace{1}_{\text{stop}} = 10 \text{ bits per byte on the wire} \nonumber
\end{align}

\subsection{The Math}

\[
\text{Bytes/sec} = \frac{\text{Baud rate}}{10}
\]

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{|r|r|r|}
\hline
\textbf{Baud Rate} & \textbf{Correct ($\div 10$)} & \textbf{Wrong ($\div 8$)} \\\hline
115,200 & 11,520\,B/s & 14,400\,B/s \\
2,000,000 & 200,000\,B/s & 250,000\,B/s \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\caption{Correct vs.\ incorrect serial throughput calculations}
\end{table}

\subsection{Utilization Calculations}

\begin{align}
\textbf{OpenBCI Cyton:} \quad \frac{33 \times 250}{115{,}200 / 10} &= \frac{8{,}250}{11{,}520} = \mathbf{71.6\%} \\[0.5em]
\textbf{InEar Original:} \quad \frac{56 \times 1000}{2{,}000{,}000 / 10} &= \frac{56{,}000}{200{,}000} = \mathbf{28.0\%} \\[0.5em]
\textbf{InEar Optimized:} \quad \frac{{\sim}28{,}500}{2{,}000{,}000 / 10} &= \frac{28{,}500}{200{,}000} = \mathbf{14.3\%}
\end{align}
